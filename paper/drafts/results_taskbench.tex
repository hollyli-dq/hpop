\subsection{External validation of the partial-order component}
\label{sec:taskbench}

The experiment in this section tests one part of HPOP on external data, and it
is important to be precise about which part. TaskBench supplies gold task graphs
over a fixed tool vocabulary; it does not supply latent skill-instance
boundaries, it does not supply reusable instance labels, and it contains no
recurrent repair trajectories. It therefore evaluates only the \emph{static
partial-order component} that HPOP uses internally --- whether a Bayesian
product-order representation, fitted to a handful of observed linearizations,
generalises to legal orderings it has never seen. It does not evaluate
segmentation, hierarchy, skill reuse, or full HPOP inference, and we draw no
such conclusion from it. A second scope limit follows from how the data are
built: the training and test linearizations are enumerated from the official
gold DAGs, so they are \emph{derived} orderings, not observed agent
trajectories.

\paragraph{Benchmark construction.}
We pin the Multimedia Tools subtree of \texttt{microsoft/JARVIS} at commit
\texttt{7624cf38} and take the official DAG subset. Of $5{,}555$ released
Multimedia records, $550$ are marked as DAGs and $529$ parse to independently
verified acyclic graphs; $388$ pass eligibility, and $48$ meet the registered
primary criteria (at most seven nodes, at least two edges, at least four legal
linearizations, at least one incomparable pair, no repeated tool identity).
Those $48$ are the entire eligible primary pool, well short of the registered
target of $300$; the criteria were not loosened to close the gap, and we report
the shortfall rather than relax the protocol. Graphs are split $11$ development
/ $37$ test, with structural deduplication applied before splitting so that
identical structures cannot leak across the split. Each graph is fitted under
three conditions --- \textsc{one}, \textsc{two} and \textsc{four} observed legal
training orderings --- giving $132$ graph-condition fitting problems. Evaluation
uses $588$ held-out legal orderings and $588$ illegal orderings ($318$ with a
single closure violation, $270$ with several). Gold relations are the transitive
\emph{closure} throughout; predicted closures are never compared against a gold
reduction. All eighteen construction gates pass, including byte-identical
rebuild under the same seed and a seed-sensitivity check confirming that the
gold graph set is seed-invariant while the sampled ordering selection is not.
Because \textsc{four} requires four distinct legal orderings, the test set has
$37$ graphs at \textsc{one} and \textsc{two} and $26$ at \textsc{four}, and the
paired condition curves below use the $26$ graphs present in all three.

\paragraph{Model and protocol.}
The fitted model is the static product-order frontier likelihood over
$H = h(U)$ with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 3}$; $\rho = 0.5$, $\beta = 0$,
$\epsilon = 0.02$, $d = 3$ and $\sigma_U = 1.0$ are all fixed rather than
fitted, and $\sigma_U$ was selected on development graphs by an efficiency-only
pilot before the protocol was frozen. The model sees node identities and the
observed training linearizations only; a source-level test forbids gold tokens
in the fitting path. Each problem is fitted with four chains from dispersed
prior starts, burn-in $5{,}000$, thin $5$, and convergence declared on two
consecutive checkpoint passes with $\widehat{R} \leq 1.01$ and the registered
ESS floors. All $132$ fits converged ($100\%$), in $1{,}584$~s of wall time on
eight workers.

\paragraph{More observations, better structure.}
Table~\ref{tab:taskbench-main} gives the full test table; the paired trend is
the headline. On the $26$ graphs present in every condition, the Bayesian
model's closure $F_1$ rises $0.452 \to 0.677 \to 0.902$ from \textsc{one} to
\textsc{two} to \textsc{four} (\textsc{four}$-$\textsc{one}
$= +0.450$, CI $[0.356, 0.552]$), incomparable-pair $F_1$ rises
$0.566 \to 0.647 \to 0.892$ ($+0.327$, CI $[0.256, 0.400]$), relation Brier
falls $0.130 \to 0.088 \to 0.041$ ($-0.088$, CI $[-0.101, -0.075]$), held-out
legal NLL per node falls $0.758 \to 0.704 \to 0.642$ ($-0.116$, CI
$[-0.165, -0.063]$), and AUROC rises $0.836 \to 0.907 \to 0.975$ ($+0.140$,
CI $[0.092, 0.188]$). Every step is in the expected direction and every
\textsc{four}$-$\textsc{one} interval excludes zero. Qualitatively, the model
starts by over-committing to the spurious ordering implied by a single observed
linearization and progressively releases those relations into incomparability as
further orderings arrive. Both degenerate baselines are beaten decisively in
every condition: a first-observed total order cannot represent incomparability
at all ($F_1 = 0.000$), and an antichain cannot represent order at all
($F_1 = 0.000$) and discriminates legal from illegal orderings at chance
(AUROC $0.500$).

\paragraph{The intersection baseline is the real competitor, and it wins on
point structure at low sample size.}
The honest comparison is against deterministic intersection of the observed
orders, and it does not favour the Bayesian model on point recovery. Paired over
test graphs, intersection is ahead on closure $F_1$ at \textsc{one}
($\Delta = -0.175$, CI $[-0.246, -0.104]$; $25$ losses, $9$ ties, $3$ wins) and
at \textsc{two} ($-0.112$, CI $[-0.182, -0.055]$), and the two are statistically
tied at \textsc{four} ($-0.004$, CI $[-0.010, 0.000]$, with $24$ of $26$ graphs
exactly tied). The single structural place where the Bayesian model is far ahead
is incomparable-pair $F_1$ at \textsc{one} ($+0.533$, CI $[0.472, 0.593]$,
$37/37$ wins), for the mechanical reason that one observed order forces the
intersection to a total order with no incomparable pairs at all.

Where the Bayesian model does win is uncertainty and prediction. Relation Brier
is better at \textsc{one} ($-0.122$, CI $[-0.144, -0.098]$, $36/37$ wins) and at
\textsc{two} ($-0.024$, CI $[-0.044, -0.005]$), and tied at \textsc{four}
($-0.004$, CI $[-0.011, 0.003]$). Held-out legal NLL per node is better in
\emph{every} condition with intervals excluding zero: $-0.933$
(CI $[-1.056, -0.810]$, $37/37$), $-0.335$ (CI $[-0.458, -0.218]$, $29/37$) and
$-0.126$ (CI $[-0.208, -0.050]$). Discrimination is a wash and we do not claim
otherwise: the Bayes-minus-intersection AUROC difference is $+0.008$, $-0.036$
and $-0.010$ at \textsc{one}, \textsc{two} and \textsc{four}, and every interval
covers zero. Intersection assigns illegal orderings lower probability than the
Bayesian model does --- its hard structure is simply more restrictive --- but
discrimination still ties, because the Bayesian model scores unseen \emph{legal}
orderings correspondingly higher.

\paragraph{Distance to the oracle.}
Scoring with the gold graph bounds what any fitted model could achieve here.
The oracle reaches legal NLL per node of $0.423$, $0.418$ and $0.476$ across the
three conditions and AUROC $1.000$; the Bayesian fits reach $0.738$, $0.685$ and
$0.642$ and AUROC $0.843$, $0.874$ and $0.975$. At \textsc{four} the structural
gap is nearly closed (closure $F_1$ $0.902$ against $1.000$) while a clear
predictive gap remains.

\paragraph{Verdict and scope.}
The registered classification is \textsc{external partial-order generalization
supported; bayesian advantage is uncertainty and prediction}. The static
product-order component recovers meaningful ordered and incomparable relations
from one to four observed linearizations on data it was not designed against, it
improves monotonically with more observations, and it ranks unseen legal
executions above illegal ones. Its advantage over the strongest deterministic
baseline is calibration and held-out predictive likelihood, not point structure
recovery, and it is a small benchmark: $48$ graphs of at most seven nodes, only
$21$ of them connected. None of this is evidence about segmentation, about
reusable skill instances, about recurrent repair, or about full HPOP.
